# Leisure travel and a local afternoon

**Date:** September 24, 2026.

**Question:** Why do people travel for leisure, what do they actually do once they arrive, and which of those pieces can a Greater Sacramento cultural day host?

**For:** [SacPassport](Sac Cultural Staycations.md). The public line is already “Where would you like to staycation?” This note is the reason that line can be honest.

## Short answer

A leisure trip is a short ritual break from the ordinary week. Inside that break, Americans mostly want time with people they already love, a change of scenery, and food that belongs to the place. Music, dance, and a walk through someone else’s gathering sit a step behind those, and they are still a large share of what people plan to do.

SacPassport can host the visitor’s afternoon: a seated meal with time to stay, something live, a place that feels like somewhere, a photo and a story. Scenery is the strongest pull. Architecture is the next built version of that pull. On a staycation, elaborate decor and costume can carry the architecture, and they work better beside a river, in a park, or up in the hills, where the setting is already doing part of the job. A dinner show seats the guest and lets the meal last. A festival can do the same inside a large crowd if it keeps a room for that curated meal. A line of food trucks, eaten standing, moves people through and drops the rest a leisure trip is for.

## Why they leave

People rarely travel for one reason, and they are bad at naming the real one when asked (Crompton, *Annals of Tourism Research*, 1979). Half a century of that research collapses into six motives that actually drive a pleasure trip (Crompton and Petrick, *Journal of Travel Research*, 2024):

| Motive | What it feels like on a trip | What a local cultural day can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curiosity | See how other people live, eat, and gather | Strong. One culture, one afternoon, a new dish and a dance you could not name that morning. |
| Boredom alleviation | Get out of the week you already know | Partial. A few hours, then the same bed. The gate and the drive help. A week away helps more. |
| Disinhibited play | Dance, eat with your hands, stay later than a Tuesday allows | Strong, if the event invites guests in. Watching from the edge is weaker. |
| Biophilia | Water, trees, weather, a view that is not the freeway | Weak. A parish lot and a high-school gym do not substitute for a coast or a mountain. Parks win this motive. |
| Bonds with the people you came with | The trip is an excuse to be with a spouse, kids, parents, friends | Strong. Festivals are built for arriving together. |
| Ego-enhancement | A story, a photo, “I went” | Partial. “I went to the Greek festival” is a real story. “I have been to Greece” is a different story. A stamp plays at the second and delivers the first. |

Two older frames still sort the same facts.

Iso-Ahola (1982) treats every leisure trip as two forces at once: escaping the personal week or the usual social circle, and seeking a personal reward (rest, mastery, a culture) or a social one (the people you came with, or strangers who are glad you came). A festival is mostly seeking. Escape is the shorter part.

Pearce and Lee (*Journal of Travel Research*, 2005) found a core that shows up for almost every traveler: escape and relaxation, stronger relationships, and some self-development. More experienced travelers add two things the core does not require: time in nature, and involvement with the people who live at the destination. Culture is a motive that grows with practice. It is not the reason a first trip exists.

Graburn (“Tourism: The Sacred Journey,” in *Hosts and Guests*, 1989) is the piece that matters for the product name. A vacation works like a small ritual. Ordinary time stops, a threshold is crossed, and then ordinary time starts again. The flight, the hotel key, and the first morning in a different city are the threshold. A festival has a thinner one: a ticket, a parking lot, a tent. SacPassport’s globe is an attempt to thicken that threshold so choosing Greece on a screen feels a little like leaving.

## What they do after they arrive

Motives are the push. The day itself is a short list of activities, and the list is more ordinary than the word “travel” suggests.

MMGY’s *Portrait of American Travelers*, Winter 2024 (4,500 U.S. adults surveyed October–November 2024; 3,633 active leisure travelers), asks what influences the choice of a place. Among active leisure travelers, top-two-box influence in the fourth quarter of 2024:

| Influence on where to go | Share |
| --- | --- |
| Beautiful scenery | 75% |
| Safety | 60% |
| Food and drink scene | 60% |
| Outdoor and nature activities | 55% |
| Historical significance | 50% |
| Family activities | 35% |
| A place the family has always gone | 32% |
| Music scene | 25% |
| Ethnic diversity and a multicultural population | 25% |
| Nightlife | 23% |

The same study’s activities of interest, same quarter:

| Activity | Share |
| --- | --- |
| Beach | 58% |
| State or national park | 55% |
| Historical sites | 53% |
| Shopping | 48% |
| Museum | 47% |
| Food tour | 37% |
| Concert or music festival | 35% |
| Guided tour into a local experience that is hard to reach alone | 33% |
| Cooking, wine, or cocktail class | 27% |
| Performing arts | 22% |
| Exploring family ancestry | 22% |
| Film or art festival | 19% |

Food is tied with safety, behind only scenery, as a reason to pick a destination. Notable architecture is a planned activity for 36%, just under a food tour (37%) and level with a concert or music festival (35%). “The place is multicultural” influences about one in four, the same share as the music scene. Younger travelers in this study lean toward other cultures and new cuisines. Older travelers lean toward friends and family. Scenery and parks sit on top for every generation. The staycation reading, below, treats decor and costume as the local stand-in for that architecture line, and a seated meal as the way food becomes a trip instead of a snack.

Deloitte’s 2024 summer travel survey (adults planning a leisure trip with paid lodging) found the same shape from the other side. People who said they were going “to rest and relax” still planned in-destination activities at the same rate as travelers overall. In 2024, about 49% expected an outdoor or adventure activity, 48% a major attraction, 34% a guided sightseeing tour, and 28% a ticketed public event such as a festival or concert. Rest is the motive. A scheduled afternoon is still the method.

Longwoods International’s traveler sentiment study, fielded February 2025, asked Americans to describe travel inside the United States. Sixty-one percent pointed to a range of food and music. Fifty-seven percent agreed that a domestic trip offers cultural traditions and places they would not otherwise see. The staycation pitch is already inside how people talk about ordinary U.S. trips. It asks them to take that description inside one metro, on a day they drive home from.

Inbound visitors to the United States show the script from the guest’s side. In the National Travel and Tourism Office’s 2024 Survey of International Air Travelers, vacation was the main purpose for 56.5% of international air arrivals and visiting friends or relatives for 22.9%. Once here, 83.1% shopped and 77.4% went sightseeing. Parks (35.6%) and museums (29.8%) follow. The memorable day, even for someone who flew, is often eating, looking, and buying a small thing. Deep cultural attendance is a minority slice of the same trip.

## The afternoon, in order

Put the motives and the activity lists together and a leisure day has a sequence:

1. Cross a threshold. The week pauses.
2. Arrive and know that you have arrived. A first look, a photo, a map.
3. Eat something that belongs to this place.
4. Watch, or join, music and dance.
5. Walk and look.
6. Buy or take a small token.
7. Be with the people you came with, a little looser than at home.
8. Tell the story later.

A Greek festival weekend, a Japanese bazaar, a parish food day, and a pow wow already run steps 3 through 8 in some form. A seated dinner show runs the meal, the show, the token, the company, and the story, and the ticket is a clear threshold. A temple garden or a cultural plaza runs the arrival and the walk on its own. Step 1 stays thin when the traveler sleeps at home. Food tied to the culture, plus something live, is the replicable core. The meal has to be a stretch of time in a seat. Bought in stops from a truck and eaten standing, step 3 never becomes the culinary experience people take a trip for.

## Scenery, architecture, and the room

Beautiful scenery is the top influence on where Americans go (75% in the MMGY winter 2024 table). Visiting notable architecture sits in the activity list at 36%. A staycation in Sacramento will rarely offer a foreign skyline. Two substitutions cover that gap, and they stack.

**Setting.** Put the afternoon by a river, in a park, or up in the hills. The guest still drove from home. The first look has changed. A gym on a flat arterial can host food and a show. It cannot host this part.

**Decor and costume.** Elaborate dress, a built interior, a courtyard dressed for the day: these are the architecture a one-day event can actually build. They do more work when the ground around them is already pleasant. Costume on a bare parking lot is a stage. Costume beside water, or under trees, or on a hillside, is a place.

**The meal is where rest happens.** Leisure travel includes relief and time with the people you came with. Abroad, that shows up as a table: order, stay, watch, talk, eat through a sequence. Many local festivals replace that with a queue, a tray, and a patch of curb. The crowd is large, and the guest is handled like throughput. The culinary pull is still on the flyer. The experience that would have made it travel has been edited out.

A dinner show solves the seating in one move. The table is the product. The show shares the room. The guest is a party of two or six, with a start and a duration.

A festival can use the same model at a larger size. The grounds can stay dense. Inside them, some of the capacity has to be a curated room: tables, a paced menu from that culture, a view of the performance, enough time that standing in line is the exception. The rest of the site can still sell snacks and walk-up plates. The trip, for the guest SacPassport is sending, is the seated stretch. Without that room, the festival remains a fair, and fairs are a different market.

**A class needs a quieter room than the show.** Cooking, wine, or cocktail classes are a planned vacation activity for 27% of active leisure travelers in that same MMGY table. Learning one dish means hearing the instructor, watching hands, and asking a question. A festival stage is built to cover a field. That volume makes a class beside it a pantomime. The class has to move: a side hall, a tent set back from the PA, a kitchen the music does not own. The stage stays for the people who came to watch. The class is a respite from it, and it is a different ticket. A dinner show can host a class more easily when the music is paced to the room. A festival hosts one only by giving it walls or distance.

## What copies, and what has to stay a trip

**Copies well.** Curiosity, play, and time with the people you arrived with, when the meal gives that time a seat. The live bill, the walk, the photo, the story told on Monday. Decor and costume copy the “notable architecture” activity at the scale of one afternoon. A river, a park, or a hillside copies a piece of the scenery pull. A guided first visit (the church tour at Weekend in Greece, a host at the table explaining the dishes) is the guided local experience that a third of MMGY travelers already say they want. Cohen’s recreational mode (*Sociology*, 1979) — a trip that restores you — fits a seated show better than a queue. His experiential mode, a few hours of wanting to see how other people gather, fits when the host explains what is in front of the table.

**Copies partly.** Escape, prestige, and contact with hosts. A Saturday is a real pause and a short one. Volunteers at a gate are hospitable, and they are not a week of neighborhood life. A SacPassport stamp can carry the ego-enhancement of “I went,” aimed at the festival rather than the country.

**Stays a trip.** A foreign city’s architecture at full scale, a week of weather, a different bed and the first morning, language heard for days, visiting relatives who live somewhere else, a beach with nothing scheduled, the status of a border crossing. MacCannell’s back region (*The Tourist*, 1976) stays a trip too: daily life when no one is performing it. Decor, costume, and a local river are the front region, built on purpose. Promising “this is what Greece is like” asks the guest to treat that front as the country. Promising a seated afternoon in a dressed room, on pleasant ground, stays inside what the host can build.

Visiting friends and relatives is the largest single leisure plan in American sentiment studies, ahead of beaches. SacPassport does not replace a flight to see family. It can serve the cultural afternoon that flight would have included, and it can serve the inverted case already on the slate: the relatives are here, and the gathering is theirs. For the host community the festival is their own ritual. SacPassport is a guide for the guest.

## What to tell an organizer

The domestic visitor is buying a short trip. They are not joining the association. The recommendation is the same for a festival committee, a dinner-show producer, and a temple or plaza that wants strangers on a Saturday: let a first-timer finish the afternoon without already knowing the culture.

Tell them nine things.

1. **Name the culture and the hours.** “Greek food and dancing, Saturday 11 to 8” is a trip a stranger can choose. “Annual fundraiser” is a membership call. One culture on the sign. A region, or three cultures on one stage, leaves the guest unsure which place they entered.
2. **Seat them for a meal that lasts.** Food is tied with safety, behind only scenery, as a reason Americans pick a destination. Abroad, that food is a table: time, courses or a full plate, room to talk, a show in view. Name the dishes. A dinner show is the clean version. A festival matches it by holding back part of the grounds as a curated dining room, even when the rest of the site is packed. Walk-up trucks can feed the crowd that wants to graze. They are the wrong product for the guest who came as a traveler. The Fijian night on the slate is the other caution: the ticket is public, and the dishes are not named.
3. **Put a cooking class where the stage cannot drown it.** About 27% of active leisure travelers plan a cooking, wine, or cocktail class into a vacation. One dish, taught so guests can hear and ask questions, is that activity. Festival PA is the wrong room. Move the class into a side hall, a tent set back from the speakers, or a kitchen the music does not own. Sell it as its own hour. The people on the lawn still get the show.
4. **Put something live in the room that belongs to that culture.** Dance, music, chant, a short ceremony guests may watch from the table. A generic playlist makes the stop a restaurant. About one in three leisure travelers already plans a concert or music festival into a vacation. Costume is part of this, and it is also the architecture substitute in the next item. The show and the class are both live. They need different volumes, so they need different rooms.
5. **Dress the room, and put it on good ground.** Notable architecture is a vacation activity for 36% of active leisure travelers. A one-day host builds that with decor and costume. It lands harder by a river, in a park, or in the hills, because scenery is the top pull (75%) and the site is supplying it. A bare lot can still be dressed. It starts further behind.
6. **Give them ground to walk, then a place to stop.** Booth to stage, courtyard to table, gate to garden. The walk is sightseeing. The table is where the walk ends. A third of MMGY travelers say they want a guided way into a local experience they could not reach alone: a host, twenty minutes, what the costume and the dishes mean.
7. **Mark a threshold.** A ticket, a greeting, shoes off, a person at the door who says what the next two hours are. That is the small version of leaving. A drive to the river or into the hills does some of this before the gate. A gym on a flat arterial has to build all of it on purpose.
8. **Hand them a sentence and a token.** One line they can repeat Monday, and something small to carry out. Ego-enhancement is a motive. “We watched the dance, then ate” is the sentence. A program, a sweet, a stamp does the carrying. A class adds a second sentence: “I learned to make it.”
9. **Say who they can bring.** Time with a spouse, kids, or parents is a core motive, and family activities influence about a third of destination choice. Write the afternoon so a pair or a family can see themselves in it: price for kids, a place to sit, a start time that is not midnight.

Three vessels, one afternoon:

| Vessel | What it already gives the guest | What the organizer still has to add |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Festival weekend | A show, a crowd, ground to walk, play | A seated dining room inside the crowd, with a paced menu. A second room, quiet enough for a cooking class, set back from the stage. Decor and costume. Good ground when the site can move: river, park, hills. One named culture. |
| Dinner show | A ticket, a seat, a meal with a duration, a show in the same room, an easy story | Dishes named as that culture’s food. Decor and costume if the room is plain. A pleasanter site when the booking can get one. |
| Place (temple grounds, cultural plaza) | Arrival, scenery, a walk, a world that exists on a weekday | A published guest path that ends at a table: a meal with time to stay, one explanation, one thing happening. |

The first SacPassport inventory kept to festivals because a public flyer usually proves food and a show on the same date. That was a search rule. It is a poor description of the market. A dinner show qualifies when the meal is the culture’s and the guest can sit with it. A festival qualifies when it keeps that same table inside the larger grounds. A place qualifies when a stranger can walk there and then sit down to eat on a published afternoon. A place that is only beautiful, with no meal and no guest path, stays a scenic drive. A festival that only sells walk-up plates stays a fair.

Promise the afternoon a visitor spends. Leave “this is what the country is like” unsaid. The host community can keep its own ritual in its own language. The guest path is a parallel track: a sign, a greeter, a menu, a start time.

## What this asks of the product

The pull is scenery first, then food, with architecture close behind food as something people plan to go see. A staycation covers scenery by siting (river, park, hills) and covers architecture with decor and costume. Food counts when the guest can sit and stay. The show belongs in the dining room, at a volume for that room. A cooking class is a second live hour, in a room the stage does not own. A week away remains a real trip. A large festival remains eligible if it builds both rooms.

Three product consequences follow.

The frame has to do work the geography will not do, except where the geography already does it. Choosing a culture on the globe, and treating the day as a departure, thickens a thin threshold. A drive into the hills is already a departure. A gym festival still needs the frame. Without either, the afternoon is a local listing people already know how to skip.

The promise stays at the scale of an afternoon. The Buddhist Church of Sacramento already says a visitor to the Japanese Food and Cultural Bazaar does not need a passport. That sentence is the product. Stretching it into “travel Greece” spends credibility the event cannot repay.

A sequence may matter more than a single stamp. Pearce’s core motives include a break that is long enough to feel like a break. One Saturday is curiosity. Three Saturdays in a season is closer to a trip: a route, a small accumulation, a story with chapters. Whether Sacramento guests want that accumulation is still an open question, not a finding.

## Worked example: Elk Grove Lunar New Year

Bao’s programming read, September 24, 2026, for the February 13–14, 2027 weekend at Elk Grove Park. This applies the destination list above to one festival he helps produce. It is not a change to the August 29, 2026 layout lock. That lock puts the festival floor on the three Pavilion lots closest to the building. Lake / Strauss (Zone 4) is an optional trek, not a staffed core.

The park is already the scenery. The festival has been using the parking lots and leaving the water alone.

| What travelers use to choose a place | What to do with it here |
| --- | --- |
| Beautiful scenery (75%) | Weather permitting, program the lake. Floating lanterns — on the water, as decor and as something that happens — are the version that uses the pond the lots cannot fake. Rain or wind, this drops, and the lots are the festival again. |
| Safety (60%) | A free civic park already reads as a safe destination. A water program still needs an edge plan. That is operations, not a reason to skip the lake in good weather. |
| Food and drink (60%) | Guests who are told this is a Vietnamese festival need Vietnamese food in the foreground. Go further than booths: prix fixe meals, seated, paced, named. That is the curated room inside a large free weekend. Walk-up plates can stay for the crowd that is grazing. |
| Outdoor / nature activities (55%) | Hiking, skiing, and sport are not this event. A trail in the park dressed with cultural monuments or experience stations is the nature activity that fits. |
| Historical significance (50%) | Those monuments and the demonstrations (a performance included) carry the history only if someone narrates them. A tour guide or an emcee. Objects without a voice are decor. |
| Family activities (35%) | The idea is easy. The work is organizing it so a family can find the sequence, the seats, and the hours. Many festivals list kids’ things and still make the parent invent the route. |
| A place the family returns to (32%), and the story afterward | One photographable centerpiece. Familiar enough that a returning guest recognizes the festival. Distinctive enough, year to year, that the photo is this year. “I was here” needs a single place to stand. |
| Music (25%) | Already the job of the stages. Keep it. |
| Nightlife (23%) | A February evening in Elk Grove gets cold, and the park festival is unlikely to run late enough to be nightlife. Put nightlife in a separate indoor afterparty. |

Cooking class, if it is offered, sits off the main PA, as in the section above. The lake program and the prix fixe are the two moves that change what kind of trip the weekend is. The trail, the narrator, the centerpiece, and the indoor afterparty fill the rest of the list the parking-lot floor does not touch.

## Open

- Is the audience the curious guest (a culture they do not already claim), the family looking for a Saturday together, or both? The motives pull different copy and different events.
- Does a stamp or a completed route change the feeling, or do guests only need this week’s afternoon, whatever vessel it comes in?
- Would one night in a hotel inside the region change the experience more than a better festival? The research says the different bed is a large part of “I left.” Nobody has tested that against a parish festival.
- Diaspora guests and non-diaspora guests may be buying different things at the same gate: kin and memory, or curiosity and play. The slate does not yet distinguish them.
